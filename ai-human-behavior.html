AI / HCI research route

Human influence mechanisms for AI builders

AI systems increasingly participate in decisions, recommendations, interfaces and conversations that can shape what people notice, believe, choose and do. That makes human influence a systems question, not only a psychology question.

The useful distinction

Capability is not intent. Influence is not automatically manipulation. A changed decision does not by itself prove coercion, deception or exploitation. The relevant analysis starts by separating the observable mechanism from assumptions about motive, then asking what the system makes easier, harder, more salient or less visible.

This is the same boundary discipline used by Manipulation — The Fool and the Wise: similar-looking outcomes should not be collapsed into one category merely because they produce the same behavior.

Why this matters in human-AI systems

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework treats human-AI interaction as part of AI risk management and explicitly notes that complex human and social phenomena can lose important context when converted into measurable representations. It also highlights the need to define human roles and responsibilities and to account for biases that can enter across design, development, deployment and use.

For builders, that creates a practical question: when an AI system recommends, ranks, frames, summarizes, nudges, defaults, withholds, personalizes or repeatedly reinforces an option, what mechanism is actually operating on the user?

Influence becomes a design property

In a digital system, persuasion can be distributed across copy, ranking, timing, defaults, friction, repetition, interface hierarchy and personalization. The FTC's work on dark patterns documents interfaces that can obscure, subvert or impair consumer autonomy, decision-making or choice. That does not make every optimized interface manipulative; it does make interface structure part of the evidence.

The EU AI Act adds a separate legal frame by prohibiting specified uses of subliminal, purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques, and specified exploitation of vulnerabilities, when the regulation's conditions for materially distorted behavior and significant harm are met. Legal thresholds are not interchangeable with psychological definitions, but they show why precise classification matters.

What to inspect

  • Information state: What does the user know, not know, or reasonably believe?
  • Choice architecture: Which options are prominent, defaulted, delayed, buried or costly to reject?
  • Source and identity: Is the apparent source independent, authentic and correctly represented?
  • Personalization: Is behavior being adapted using inferred vulnerability, urgency, emotional state or prior compliance?
  • Feedback: Does the system learn which framing produces acquiescence and intensify it?
  • Exit and correction: Can the user revise, refuse, compare, inspect provenance or recover from a mistaken assumption?

What the book contributes—and what it does not

The book organizes mechanisms of manipulation, persuasion, deception, pressure and related forms of influence into a source-mapped reference system. It distinguishes documented mechanisms from editorial synthesis and hypothesis.

It does not establish that every psychological mechanism maps cleanly to an AI feature, that software can reliably infer intent, or that a proposed AI product is technically or commercially validated. Those are separate questions.

Phenomenon exists → software can address it → deployment is justified are three different claims.

Operational questions for builders

  • What observable action or interface event are we classifying?
  • Which human mechanism could plausibly explain the effect?
  • What alternative explanations remain compatible with the same observation?
  • What would distinguish assistance from steering in this context?
  • What evidence would be required before inferring vulnerability, intent or coercion?
  • Can the user inspect, contest or reverse the system's framing?
  • If optimization succeeds, what secondary behavior does it reward?
